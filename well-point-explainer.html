A driven well, explained

The well you drive with a sledgehammer

01

What kind of well this is

A sand-point well — also called a sandpoint, driven-point, or shallow-point well — is not drilled. It is driven. A pointed, screened steel assembly is hammered into the ground until the screen sits below the water table, and a suction pump lifts water up through the same pipe.

Think of a straw pushed into wet sand. The pipe is both the casing and the supply line; there is no separate borehole, no rig, no gravel pack. The whole thing goes in with hand tools, and in the video it takes a day.

Three conditions have to line up: sand or gravel that water can move through, a water table shallow enough to reach, and a pump that can lift from that depth. In the video the builder drives 20 ft through New England soil, meets water at roughly 12 ft, and finishes with a hand pump.

02

The build, in cross-section

One pipe does three jobs: it is the casing, the riser, and the drive rod. The screen is the intake. The point is the tool. Everything above ground is a pump — and everything below the water table is the reason it works.

03

The parts, by name

Most of the vocabulary is plumbing. These are the pieces that matter, and the small design details that make a driven well different from a drilled one.

Sand point also sandpoint, well point, drive point
A four-foot steel assembly: cast-iron point, perforated pipe, stainless mesh screen. It is both the intake and the driving tip.
Well screen
The mesh-wrapped section that lets water in and keeps formation sand out. Its openings have to suit the soil: too fine and it plugs, too coarse and it pumps grit.
Riser
The 1¼-inch galvanized pipe that carries water up. In a driven well it is also the casing — there is no separate liner.
Drive coupling also well coupling
A heavy coupling threaded at both ends, with the threads cut short of each end. The exposed shoulder takes the hammer blow so the threads do not.
Smash cap also hammer cap, drive cap
A sacrificial steel cap that threads onto the top of the pipe and absorbs the sledge. It is meant to be beaten, not saved.
Cheater pipe
A short section added below the cap. It deforms instead of the working threads — in the video it bent slightly and the rest of the string survived.
Pipe dope
Thread sealant for the joints. It has to be rated for potable water. The video uses one that is, and switches to tape at the pump.
Post-hole digger
A manual auger. It starts a hole about four feet deep, clears roots and rocks, and keeps the first pipe vertical.
Pitcher pump
A hand-operated suction pump. Simple and repairable, good for about 20 ft of lift. Its leather cup seals need soaking before first use.
Priming
Pouring water into the pump to wet the leather and start the vacuum. A dry pump just moves air.
Water table also static water level
The top of the saturated zone. Its depth is the single biggest yes-or-no for a sand point.
Suction lift
The vertical distance from the water surface to the pump. A vacuum can raise water about 25 ft at sea level, less at altitude — the hard ceiling on this kind of well.°
Submergence
How far the screen sits below the water table. The video adds a full section after hitting water so the point does not suck air as the level drops.
Drawdown
The drop in water level while the pump is running. If it falls below the screen, the well starts pulling air instead of water.
Well development
Pumping out the fines until the water runs clear. It took about 15 minutes in the video; fine soils can take much longer.
Sanitary seal
A grouted, sealed connection between casing and ground. A driven point generally has none, which is the main reason its water is more exposed to surface contamination.°

The sequence

Eight moves, in order. The timestamps open the relevant part of the source video.

  1. 01

    Clean the pipe

    New galvanized pipe can carry cutting oil inside. The video pulls a soapy brush through each section on a rope, then rinses. Even for garden water, the inside of the pipe should be clean.

    Watch 1:18

  2. 02

    Choose the ground

    He sites it a few feet from a shed — room for a future electric pump — and on a lower part of the yard, which puts him closer to the water table. Call the utility locator first, and stay away from the septic field and downhill drainage.

    Watch 1:25

  3. 03

    Start the hole

    A post-hole digger opens about four feet. It removes roots and rocks that would deflect the pipe, and gives the first section somewhere to stand.

    Watch 2:13

  4. 04

    Assemble the string

    Point, coupling, pipe. Dope both threads, hand-tighten, then bring two pipe wrenches. The video’s advice: you cannot make these too tight — the string is meant to be beaten.

    Watch 2:48

  5. 05

    Cap and drive

    Thread on the smash cap, add the cheater pipe, and work the sledge. Add a section whenever the pipe gets low. Stop periodically to re-tighten the couplings and the cap; hammering loosens both, and a loose joint eats the force.

    Watch 3:36

  6. 06

    Stop at water, then keep going

    Start checking around 12 ft. When the pipe meets saturated sand, add at least one more full section so the screen finishes below the water table.

    Watch 5:17

  7. 07

    Set the pump

    Soak the pump’s leather gaskets in a bucket for an hour first. Tape the threads, screw the pump on, and tighten.

    Watch 5:55

  8. 08

    Prime and develop

    Pour water down the top, then pump. It takes a while to draw from depth. The first water is dirty; keep going until it clears.

    Watch 6:26

05

What decides whether it works

A driven point is less a construction project than a bet on the ground. These are the variables that settle it. Items marked ° are general well practice added for context; they are not claims the video makes.

Chance, then geology

Sand and gravel, not clay or rock. A driven point needs formation it can enter and water it can move through. Local drillers, a neighbour’s sand point, and USGS groundwater maps are better evidence than a yard that looks right. The video is blunt: you do not know until you drive.

Depth

The screen has to be below the water table, and the water table has to be within suction lift. At about 25 ft, a surface pump stops being an option; that is a different well.°

Legal

The video notes that sand points are often exempt from well rules. Often is not always. Check the town and state before driving; some places require a permit, setbacks, or restrict potable use.

Contamination

Shallow water is surface-influenced. Septic fields, road salt, fuel tanks, and farm runoff all matter. Site uphill and away, and test before drinking.

Yield

A 1¼-inch pipe and a small screen do not deliver much. Expect garden-scale flow, not a household supply. Multiple points can be joined to one pump.°

Screen clogging

Fine sand and silt can plug the mesh. Development clears some of it; very fine formations may never run clean.

Frost

The pump and the exposed riser need draining or protection. A hand pump left full of water can split.°

Damage

Threads loosen under hammering, cheater pipes bend, and a rock can stop the pipe for good. If it happens, the video’s only recovery is to work the pipe loose, pull what you can, and try another spot.

06

Water, before you drink it

Before you drink

Clear water is not the same as safe water.

The video is explicit: have the water tested before you drink it. A shallow point draws from the first saturated layer, close to the surface, and a driven well of this kind usually has no grout seal around the casing. Septic fields, road salt, fuel, and farm runoff are all within reach.

At minimum, test for total coliform and E. coli, plus nitrate. Add arsenic, lead, PFAS, or radon depending on your region. Until a lab says otherwise, treat it as irrigation water — which, as the video points out, is still most of what a household uses water for.

07

What it is not

  • Not a drilled well. It will not cut through bedrock, and it does not reach deep aquifers. If the water is 40 ft down, this is the wrong tool.
  • Not guaranteed. Any well is a gamble; a driven point is a cheap one. A rock or a dry layer can end the attempt, and the video says so plainly.
  • Not permit-free by default. Exemptions for sand points are common, not universal. Verify locally before the first strike.
  • Not potable by default. A clear glass is not a lab result.
  • Not high-yield. Low flow is the trade for low cost and a one-day install.

None of that makes it a bad well. It makes it a specific tool: a low-cost, low-yield, hand-powered supply for a garden, a few animals, or an emergency — installed in a day, and entirely dependent on ground you can actually drive into.
